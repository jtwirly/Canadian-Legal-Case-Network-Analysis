This analysis 10,867 SCC decisions from 1877 to 2026 with full text, which allowed for extracting actual citation links, judge panels, subjects, and provincial origins.

Five panels:

**Citation network (top)** — 918 nodes, 2,607 directed edges among the most-cited cases and their citing neighbours. X-axis is the year of decision; Y-axis is PageRank. The upward-right cluster (high-PageRank, recent) shows cases that are both heavily cited and themselves cite authoritative precedent. *Housen v. Nikolaisen* (2002, 131 citations — appellate standards of review) dominates; *Dunsmuir* (2008, 87 — admin law) and *Bell ExpressVu* (2002, 106 — statutory interpretation) are the other apex nodes. The long left tail is the historical corpus: many decisions but sparse internal connections because older SCR citations are harder to parse into the same index.

**Judge co-occurrence (mid-left)** — 42 judges with ≥50 cases post-1980. McLachlin is the central hub, which reflects her 37-year tenure across multiple Chief Justiceships. Lamer, La Forest, Cory, and Iacobucci form a tight Dickson-era cluster; Wagner and Karakatsanis are the current-era core.

**Most-cited cases (mid-right)** — *Housen* is a procedural case (standards of appellate review — applicable to almost every appeal), which explains its outsized citation count. The list is a useful reading list of SCC jurisprudential anchors: admin law (*Dunsmuir*, *Khosa*, *Vavilov*), criminal procedure (*Grant*, *Proulx*), constitutional (*Bedford*, *Carter*).

**Decision length by decade (bottom-left)** — The length explosion since the 1990s is striking. Median decisions in the 1920s were ~2,000 words; by the 2010s, ~10,000. Volume peaked in the 1990s–2000s and has since declined — the Court has been granting fewer leaves and deciding fewer cases but writing more per decision.

**Provincial origins (bottom-right)** — Ontario and Quebec are near-equal at ~2,500 appeals each, reflecting the Court's bilingual mandate. The FK colour shows Quebec decisions are marginally harder to read (12.0) than Ontario ones (11.2), likely the bilingual/civil law drafting style. Alberta and Saskatchewan lean greener — shorter, more readable decisions on average.
